Open Call for Research Collaboration

Teeth as Witnesses to What Happens to a Person

Six years studying why teeth break down, not from the standpoint of cavities and hygiene, but from what is happening to a person psychologically at the same time.

I have a working methodology (patent, 220+ diagnostic cases, an active practice), my own psychometric instrument, and accumulated data linking specific patterns of interpersonal relationships to the location and character of dental pathology. I'm currently a master's student in psychology (CBT track), and I plan to keep working in this field: the connection between teeth and relationships.

I am not a biologist and don't do laboratory science. I don't have access to the equipment needed to test causal mechanisms rather than correlations. So this letter is not a pitch and not an ask. It's an open entry point: if what's described below interests you, here are the contacts; from there we can figure out together who needs what.

This letter isn't for people who already have the full answer to why teeth break down. Textbooks and clinical guidelines answer that question exactly as far as they answer it, and no further. If the question is closed for you, there's no need to read on. This is for people who allow that something in this area hasn't been discovered yet, and who find more interest in testing an unconventional hypothesis than in defending the existing picture.

What I see in the data

Working across hundreds of cases, I see a consistent link between:

  • specific patterns of manipulative and codependent relationships (who dominates, who is idealized, who is rejected), and
  • which teeth break down, on which side, and in what sequence.

This isn't "stress affects health in general." It's more specific: different types of relational pressure correspond to different patterns of damage. Consistent enough to be a hypothesis, not a coincidence.

Questions I can't close on my own

  1. Why MIH occurs (molar incisor hypomineralization), and why it isn't fully explained by known environmental factors alone.
  2. Why the damage is asymmetric: if this were a purely environmental factor distributed through the whole body, where does the sidedness come from?
  3. Why teeth keep breaking down even when external factors (hygiene, diet) are controlled: what sustains the process?
  4. What can be done about it: not just stopping the breakdown, but understanding where the line falls between remineralization (which teeth can already do) and actual tissue restoration or induced tissue growth (which is currently being researched in laboratories, not by me).
To be clear on point four: I don't claim teeth can regrow. Enamel is dead tissue once a tooth erupts, it can't heal itself. Real tooth regeneration research (growing whole new teeth from dormant buds, USAG-1 antibody trials by the Takahashi group / Toregem BioPharma, and pulp/dentin stem cell work) is happening in labs, not by me. My focus is different: the psychological and stress context around teeth, both before they break down and while it's happening.

Where I think it converges

The HPA axis and enamel formation windows. Permanent central incisors form in a window that coincides in time with certain developmental crises of attachment; third molars form later. If chronic stress programs the HPA axis and simultaneously affects amelogenesis, the tooth is not a cause and not a participant; it's a witness that can't be erased or talked into silence. This could be tested using models of chronic stress, including in primates, where patterns of social stress and hierarchy are close enough to human ones to make the results more interesting than in rodents.

Equipment I don't have. fMRI: to observe what happens when presenting stimuli tied to specific relationship figures (I already have a protocol and EEG/HRV data from my own equipment showing a difference in stress index between blocks). Microscopy and histology: to see what distinguishes a "stress-type" lesion from an ordinary one at the tissue level.

Data that labs don't have. I have a sample of people with diagnosed relationship patterns and documented dental pathology over time, the kind of data that's hard to assemble from scratch.

Who this might interest

  • Researchers working with primates and chronic stress models: if the physiological bridge between social stress and hard tooth tissue formation is of interest.
  • Researchers in regenerative dentistry (pulp, dentin, growth induction): as a source of hypotheses about who, and under what conditions, regeneration will be harder or easier to trigger.
  • Anyone with access to equipment (fMRI, microscopy) interested in an unconventional hypothesis backed by practical data, not just theory.
  • Investors looking at long-horizon research bets rather than a finished product.
